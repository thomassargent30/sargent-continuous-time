## Introduction

Chapter 17 developed the theory of linear prediction for a process that is observed
*continuously* in time. Working in the Hilbert space $L^2$, it represented a stationary process
as a convolution of a square-integrable kernel with an orthogonal random measure, exploited the
isometry between functions in $L^2$ and the random variables they generate, and showed that the
least-squares forecast is obtained on the function side by a simple operation on the kernel.

In practice, however, economic variables are almost never observed continuously. We have
quarterly observations on GNP, monthly observations on many prices, and so on, even though there
is no reason to think that the economy moves only at the dates on which we happen to collect
data. *Temporal aggregation* is the name we give to this situation: an underlying process evolves
in continuous time, but we observe it only at discrete intervals.

This chapter studies what is lost in that step. We keep the continuous-time framework of
Chapter 17 — a random measure with orthogonal, second-moment-stationary increments, and processes
built as convolutions of $L^2$ kernels with that measure — and we ask how the fundamental moving
average representation (MAR, or Wold representation) of the *sampled* process relates to the MAR
of the underlying *continuous* process. Because the MAR summarizes the entire linear forecasting
structure of a process, this is the natural object through which to view the effects of sampling.

The questions we focus on are the following. Which features of the continuous MAR are captured by
the discrete MAR, and which are systematically distorted? How are the one-step-ahead prediction
errors in continuous and discrete time related? What happens when, in addition to sampling, the
data are *averaged* over the sampling interval? And can the continuous model be recovered by
sampling ever more finely?

The answers are useful in practice. If an estimated discrete-time model has features that seem
hard to reconcile with economic theory, one would like to know whether temporal aggregation alone
could have produced them. If so, the remedy is either to find data at a finer interval or to
estimate a continuous-time structural model from discrete data, using the cross-equation
restrictions of a rational expectations model (Hansen and Sargent (1980b, 1981d)).

The approach taken here, following Sims (1971) and Geweke (1978), is most cleanly expressed in the
language of $L^2$. The central technical idea is exactly the projection idea of Chapter 17: the
discrete-time forecast, viewed on the function side, is the projection of the continuous-time
kernel onto the subspace generated by its integer shifts. Almost everything below is a consequence
of that one observation.

## 1. Setup and Notation

Let $y : \mathbb{R} \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be an $n$-dimensional continuous-time process
on a probability space $(\Omega, S, P)$. We treat random variables as elements of the Hilbert
space with inner product $E(xz)$ and distance $d(x,z) = [E(x-z)^2]^{1/2}$, so that two random
variables are identified when they are equal with probability one. For $s \le t$, let $H_y(t)$ be
the closed linear subspace spanned by $\{y_i(s) : s \le t,\ i = 1,\dots,n\}$; intuitively it
contains all limits of linear combinations $\sum_k \mu_k' y(t_k)$ with $t_k \le t$. The
projection of $y(t+\alpha)$ onto $H_y(t)$, denoted $\eta(y(t+\alpha)\mid H_y(t))$, is the best
linear predictor of $y(t+\alpha)$ given information through $t$.

We assume throughout that $y$ has mean zero, finite variances, is covariance stationary, and is
linearly regular. These assumptions guarantee a fundamental moving average representation

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-mar-cont
y(t) = \int_0^\infty a(u)\, \zeta(t - du),
```

where $a : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ is square integrable and $\zeta$ is an
$m$-dimensional orthonormal random measure. The defining property of the random measure is the
isometry that played a central role in Chapter 17: for square-integrable $f, g$,

$$
E\!\left[ \int f\, d\zeta \cdot \int g'\, d\zeta \right] = \int f \cdot g',
$$

from which the autocovariance function of $y$ is
$E[y(t) y(t-s)'] = \int_0^\infty a(u) a(u-s)'\, du$. The MAR also delivers the forecast directly:

$$
\eta(y(t)\mid H_y(t-\alpha)) = \int_\alpha^\infty a(u)\, \zeta(t - du), \qquad \alpha > 0.
$$

From $y$ we define the **sampled process** $Y : I \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^n$, where $I$ is the
set of integers, by

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-sample
Y(t) = y(t) \qquad \text{for all } t \in I.
```

The sampled process inherits the good properties of $y$: it is plainly stationary with mean zero,
and because $H_Y(s) \subset H_y(s)$ for each integer $s$, the variance of its $s$-back projection
is dominated by that of $y$ and hence also vanishes as $s \to -\infty$, so $Y$ is linearly
regular. Therefore $Y$ has a discrete MAR

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-mar-disc
Y(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty A_k\, \epsilon(t-k),
```

with $A_k$ an $n \times n$ matrix and $\epsilon$ an $n$-dimensional white noise. (We extend $a$
and $A$ by zero to negative arguments, so that {eq}`eq-ta-mar-cont` and {eq}`eq-ta-mar-disc` may be
written as convolutions $y = a * \zeta$ and $Y = A * \epsilon$.) Unlike $\zeta$, the components of
$\epsilon$ need not be contemporaneously uncorrelated.

We assume from now on that $y$ has full rank — equivalently, that its spectral density is positive
definite at almost every frequency. The sampled process is then full rank as well, because its
spectral density is given by the **folding formula**

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-folding
f_Y(\omega) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty f_y(\omega + 2\pi k),
```

which is positive definite almost everywhere whenever $f_y$ is.

## 2. The Innovation of the Sampled Process

Normalize the discrete MAR so that $\epsilon(t) = Y(t) - \eta(Y(t)\mid H_Y(t-1))$ is the
one-step-ahead innovation. As Hansen and Sargent (1984) observed, this innovation decomposes as

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-eps-decomp
\epsilon(t) = \int_0^1 a(u)\, \zeta(t-du) + B_t,
\qquad
B_t = \eta(y(t)\mid H_y(t-1)) - \eta(Y(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)).
```

The two pieces are orthogonal: $B_t \in H_y(t-1)$, while the first term is the continuous-time
one-step innovation, which is orthogonal to $H_y(t-1)$. In words, the discrete innovation is the
continuous-time innovation *plus* the error made in predicting the continuous-time forecast from
discrete data. Since $B_t \in H_\zeta(t-1)$, there is a kernel $c$ — equal to $a$ on $[0,1)$ — with

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-eps-c
\epsilon(t) = \int_0^\infty c(u)\, \zeta(t-du).
```

There is a useful reading of $B_t$. By the law of iterated projections,
$\eta(Y(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) = \eta[\eta(y(t)\mid H_y(t-1)) \mid H_Y(t-1)]$, so $B_t$ is the error made
in predicting the *continuous-time* forecast of $y(t)$ from discrete data. The sampled innovation
is large, relative to the continuous one, precisely when the continuous-time forecast is hard to
reconstruct from samples.

One immediate consequence of {eq}`eq-ta-eps-c` is that the discrete innovation $\epsilon(t)$ is in
general correlated with continuous-time innovations that occurred before $t-1$: sampling spreads
the innovation backward in time. Hansen and Sargent (1984) give an explicit characterization of $c$
for the case in which $Y$ has an autoregressive representation, a case taken up in the appendix.

To characterize $c$, write the discrete forecast itself as
$\eta(Y(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) = \int_1^\infty h(u)\, \zeta(t-du)$, so that $c = a - h$. The function $h$
has a clean description in $L^2$. Endow the space $L_n^2$ of row-vector functions
$f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with the inner product
$\langle f, g\rangle = \int f(u) g(u)'\, du$, and let $a_i$ be the $i$-th row of $a$ (each $a_i$
lies in $L_n^2$, since $\|a_i\|^2 = \operatorname{var}(y_i(t)) < \infty$). Define the closed
subspace

$$
A = \operatorname{cl}\Big\{ f \in L_n^2 : f(u) = \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^s \sum_{j=1}^n \mu_k^j\, a_j(u-k)
\ \text{for finite } s,\ \mu_k^j \in \mathbb{R} \Big\},
$$

the closed span of the integer-lagged rows of $a$. The following is the central result.

```{admonition} Proposition 1
:class: note
For each $i = 1,\dots,n$, $\;h_i = \eta(a_i \mid A)$, the projection of $a_i$ onto $A$ in $L_n^2$.
```

*Proof.* Fix $i$. Since $A$ is a closed linear subspace of a Hilbert space, the projection
$\eta(a_i\mid A)$ exists and is the unique element of $A$ for which $a_i - \eta(a_i\mid A)$ is
orthogonal to every $f \in A$. We verify that $h_i$ has this property. Using the isometry between
$L_n^2$ and the random variables generated by $\zeta$, and the identity
$\int_0^\infty a_j(u-k)\,\zeta(t-du) = Y_j(t-k)$,

$$
\int_0^\infty [a_i(u) - h_i(u)]\, a_j(u-k)'\, du
= \operatorname{cov}\big[\,Y_i(t) - \eta(Y_i(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)),\ Y_j(t-k)\,\big] = 0
$$

for all $k \ge 1$ and all $j$, because the one-step innovation is orthogonal to past $Y$'s. Hence
$a_i - h_i$ is orthogonal to every generator of $A$, and so to all of $A$. It remains to show
$h_i \in A$. By definition of the discrete forecast there are finite combinations
$z_\nu = \sum_{k,j} \lambda_{k,j}^\nu Y_j(t-k)$ with $\operatorname{var}[\eta(Y_i(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) - z_\nu] \to 0$.
Setting $f_\nu(u) = \sum_{k,j} \lambda_{k,j}^\nu a_j(u-k) \in A$, the same isometry gives
$\|h_i - f_\nu\|^2 = \operatorname{var}[\eta(Y_i(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) - z_\nu] \to 0$, so $f_\nu \to h_i$
and, $A$ being closed, $h_i \in A$. Therefore $h_i = \eta(a_i\mid A)$. $\blacksquare$

This is precisely the projection idea of Chapter 17, now carried out on the kernel: the
discrete-time forecast corresponds, on the function side, to approximating $a_i$ as closely as
possible by a combination $\sum_k \lambda_k\, a(u-k)$ of its own integer shifts. The residual of
that approximation is $c_i = a_i - \eta(a_i\mid A)$. Once $h$ is known, $c = a - h$ is determined.

The following equivalences, used repeatedly below, are immediate from this characterization.

```{admonition} Proposition 2
:class: note
For each $i$, the following are equivalent: (i) $c_i(u) = 0$ for almost every $u \ge 1$;
(ii) $\eta(Y_i(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) = \eta(y_i(t)\mid H_y(t-1))$, i.e. discrete and continuous
one-step forecasts of $y_i$ coincide; (iii) $a_i(u) = \eta(a_i\mid A)(u)$ for almost every
$u \ge 1$.
```

## 3. The MAR of the Sampled Process

We can now express the discrete MAR coefficients in terms of the continuous kernel.

```{admonition} Proposition 3
:class: note
The matrices in the discrete MAR {eq}`eq-ta-mar-disc` are
```

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-Ak
A_k = \left[ \int_0^\infty a(u+k)\, c(u)'\, du \right]
      \left[ \int_0^\infty c(u)\, c(u)'\, du \right]^{-1},
\qquad k = 0, 1, \dots,
```

with $c = a - \eta(a\mid A)$.

*Proof.* Let $V = E[\epsilon(t)\epsilon(t)']$. Expressing $Y$ in its MAR and using
{eq}`eq-ta-eps-c`,

$$
A_k V = E[Y(t+k)\,\epsilon(t)'] = \int_0^\infty a(u)\, c(u-k)'\, du = \int_0^\infty a(u+k)\, c(u)'\, du,
$$

while $V = \int_0^\infty c(u) c(u)'\, du$, which is invertible because $y$ is full rank.
$\blacksquare$

Proposition 3 says that $A_k$ is a weighted average of the continuous kernel $a$ over $[k,\infty)$,
with $c$ supplying the weights. Two facts shape those weights. First, $c = a$ on $[0,1)$, and one
can show that every function in $A$ vanishes on $[0,1)$, so the projection contributes nothing
there. Second, on $[1,\infty)$ the size of $c$ is governed by

$$
\int_1^\infty \|c_i\|^2
= \operatorname{var}\!\big[\eta(Y_i(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) - \eta(y_i(t)\mid H_y(t-1))\big],
$$

the gap between the discrete and continuous one-step forecasts. When discrete data forecast nearly
as well as continuous data, $c$ is small on $[1,\infty)$, so $A_k$ depends mostly on values of $a$
in the interval $[k, k+1)$ and the discrete kernel resembles the continuous one. The typical shape
of $c$ — concentrated on $[0,1)$, small thereafter — is shown in {numref}`fig-ta-c`.

```{figure} figures/fig-18-1.png
:name: fig-ta-c
:width: 60%
:align: center

The weighting kernel $c$. It coincides with $a$ on $[0,1)$ and is small on $[1,\infty)$ when
discrete data predict almost as well as continuous data.
```

In the extreme case in which the two one-step forecasts coincide (Proposition 2), $c = 0$ on
$[1,\infty)$ and

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-Ak-special
A_k = \left[ \int_0^1 a(u+k)\, a(u)'\, du \right]\left[ \int_0^1 a(u)\, a(u)'\, du \right]^{-1},
```

so $A_k$ depends only on $a$ over $[k, k+1)$.

**A systematic bias.** Even when the discrete kernel resembles the continuous one, sampling
inflates the first coefficients. For any integer $\nu$,

$$
\operatorname{var}\!\big(Y_i(t) - \eta(Y_i(t)\mid H_Y(t-\nu))\big)
\ \ge\ \operatorname{var}\!\big(y_i(t) - \eta(y_i(t)\mid H_y(t-\nu))\big),
$$

which, in the univariate case, becomes
$\sigma_\epsilon^2 \sum_{k=0}^{\nu-1} A_k^2 \ge \int_0^\nu a(u)^2\, du$. The discrete MAR must
"pack" into a few coefficients the variance that the continuous MAR spreads over an interval, so
its early coefficients are too large in absolute value, as illustrated in {numref}`fig-ta-bias`.

```{figure} figures/fig-18-2.png
:name: fig-ta-bias
:width: 60%
:align: center

Continuous MAR $a(u)$ and discrete MAR $A_k$ plotted together: the first discrete coefficients are
systematically too large in absolute value.
```

**Contamination.** Formula {eq}`eq-ta-Ak` also shows that the $i$-th row of $A_k$ depends, through
$a$ and $c$, on *all* rows of the continuous kernel. Thus each sampled variable's MAR mixes the
continuous dynamics of every variable in the system — the phenomenon Geweke (1978) called
*contamination*. It is present even when discrete and continuous forecasts coincide, and disappears
only in the very special case $a_{ij} \equiv 0$ for all $i \ne j$, i.e. when the variables are
mutually uncorrelated at all leads and lags. Unlike the weighting kernels of the distributed-lag
problem studied by Sims and Geweke, the kernel $c$ here is one-sided and in general discontinuous.

To see the discontinuity, suppose $a$ is univariate and continuous except at zero, with
$a(0) \ne 0$. Since $c(u) = a(u) - \sum_{k \ge 1} \lambda_k\, a(u-k)$, at an integer $\nu$ only the
first $\nu$ shifts contribute, and the term $k = \nu$ contributes $\lambda_\nu\, a(0)$ — a jump,
because $a$ jumps at $0$. The remaining terms are continuous at $\nu$, so $c$ inherits a jump at
each integer. This one-sidedness and these jumps are exactly what distinguish the present problem
from the distributed-lag model: there the aggregation kernel is two-sided and continuous, and its
effect is to *shrink* the first few discrete coefficients relative to the continuous ones, whereas
here the effect is the opposite — the first discrete MAR coefficients are inflated.

### Examples

A few one-variable examples show how Propositions 1–3 are used, and when distortions are large.

**Example 3.1 (continuous AR(1): no distortion).** Let $a(u) = e^{-\lambda u}$, $\lambda > 0$.
Since $e^{-\lambda} a(u-1) = a(u)$ for all $u \ge 1$, the projection reproduces $a$ exactly on
$[1,\infty)$, so $c = 0$ there and Proposition 2 applies. Formula {eq}`eq-ta-Ak-special` gives

$$
A_k = \left[\int_0^1 e^{-\lambda(u+k)} e^{-\lambda u}\, du\right]
      \left[\int_0^1 e^{-2\lambda u}\, du\right]^{-1} = e^{-\lambda k} = a(k).
$$

The sampled process is a discrete AR(1) with parameter $e^{-\lambda}$, and the discrete MAR is
exactly the continuous MAR sampled at the integers.

**Example 3.2 (a smooth continuous kernel that is nonetheless distorted).** Take
$a(u) = e^{-\lambda_1 u} + e^{-\lambda_2 u}$ with $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$. Here
$\int_1^\infty \|c\|^2 > 0$: if it were zero, Proposition 2 would supply a constant $\mu$ with
$\mu\, a(u-1) = a(u)$ for all $u \ge 1$; evaluating at $u = 1, 2$ forces
$(e^{-\lambda_1} + e^{-\lambda_2})^2 = e^{-2\lambda_1} + e^{-2\lambda_2}$, which is impossible.
So an apparently well-behaved sum of two exponentials already yields a distorted discrete MAR.
More generally (Hansen and Sargent (1984)): if $a$ is continuous with $a(0) \ne 0$ and $Y$ has an
autoregressive representation with $c = 0$ on $[1,\infty)$, then that representation must be of
order one. Anything richer than a continuous AR(1) is distorted by sampling.

**Example 3.3 (good prediction, bad shape).** Let $|a(u)| = e^{-\lambda u}$ but let $a$ change
sign within each unit interval, so that $a$ oscillates while its envelope decays
({numref}`fig-ta-osc`). Again $e^{-\lambda} a(u-1) = a(u)$ for $u \ge 1$, so by Proposition 2 the
discrete and continuous forecasts coincide and $A_k = e^{-\lambda k} > 0$. The discrete MAR misses
the oscillations in $a$ completely. This shows that even when discrete data predict *as well as*
continuous data, the discrete MAR can look nothing like the continuous one: when $a$ takes both
signs on $[k, k+1)$, $A_k$ — a weighted average of $a$ over that interval — is not a faithful
summary of its shape.

```{figure} figures/fig-18-3.png
:name: fig-ta-osc
:width: 60%
:align: center

A kernel with $|a(u)| = e^{-\lambda u}$ that changes sign within each unit interval. The discrete
forecast is exact ($A_k = e^{-\lambda k}$), yet the discrete MAR captures none of the oscillations.
```

**Example 3.4 (engineering a distortion).** When the projection is *not* exact, the discrete MAR is
distorted whenever there is no constant $\mu$ with $a(u) = \mu\, a(u-1)$ on $[1,2)$, since then

$$
\int_1^2 c^2 = \int_1^2 \big(a(u) - \mu\, a(u-1)\big)^2\, du > 0.
$$

It is therefore easy to manufacture large distortions: choose a kernel $a$ that looks very
different on $[0,1)$ than on $[1,2)$, as in {numref}`fig-ta-construct`. The further the shape of $a$
on $[1,2)$ departs from a scaled copy of its shape on $[0,1)$, the larger $\int_1^2 c^2$ and the
more severely $A_k$ misrepresents $a$.

```{figure} figures/fig-18-4.png
:name: fig-ta-construct
:width: 60%
:align: center

A kernel whose shape on $[1,2)$ is not a scaled copy of its shape on $[0,1)$, producing a large
residual $\int_1^2 c^2$ and a badly distorted discrete MAR.
```

**Example 3.5 ($a(0) = 0$ need not mean distortion).** Example 3.2 might suggest that a continuous
kernel with $a(0) = 0$ always yields a poor discrete approximation, but this is not so. Take any
$a$ that is continuous on $[0,1]$ with $a(0) = a(1) = 0$, and extend it by $a(u) = \ell\, a(u-1)$,
$0 < \ell < 1$, for $u \ge 1$. By construction $\ell\, a(u-1) = a(u)$ on $[1,\infty)$, so
Proposition 2(ii) applies, the discrete and continuous one-step forecasts coincide, and $Y$ is a
discrete AR(1). What matters for distortion is not the value $a(0)$ but whether $a$ on $[1,2)$ is a
scaled copy of $a$ on $[0,1)$.

These examples also illustrate the **aliasing problem**: Examples 3.1, 3.3, and 3.5 exhibit
different continuous MARs that all generate the *same* sampled process (a discrete AR(1)). The
continuous kernel cannot be recovered from discrete data without further restrictions.

## 4. Granger Causality

For a bivariate continuous process in which $y_2$ does not Granger-cause $y_1$, sampling will in
general *create* Granger causality: $Y_2$ typically helps predict $Y_1$. The reason is that the
discrete forecast of $Y_1$ implicitly involves the values of $y_1$ between sampling dates, and past
$Y_2$ can help predict those intermediate values.

There is, however, a clean sufficient condition for non-causality to survive sampling.

```{admonition} Proposition 4
:class: note
If $\eta(y_1(t)\mid H_y(t-1)) = \eta(Y_1(t)\mid H_Y(t-1))$ and $y_2$ fails to Granger-cause $y_1$,
then $Y_2$ fails to Granger-cause $Y_1$.
```

*Proof.* The hypotheses give
$\eta(y_1(t)\mid H_y(t-1)) = \eta(y_1(t)\mid H_{y_1}(t-1)) = \eta(Y_1(t)\mid H_Y(t-1))$. Hence
$\eta(Y_1(t)\mid H_y(t-1)) \in H_{y_1}(t-1) \cap H_Y(t-1) = H_{Y_1}(t-1)$, which forces
$\eta(Y_1(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) = \eta(Y_1(t)\mid H_{Y_1}(t-1))$: past $Y_2$ does not help. $\blacksquare$

Intuitively, when discrete data predict $y_1$ as well as continuous data, there is nothing to gain
from predicting the intermediate values of $y_1$, and hence no room for $Y_2$ to enter.

The effect can also run the other way: one can construct continuous systems in which $y_2$ *does*
Granger-cause $y_1$ yet $Y_2$ does not Granger-cause $Y_1$. Consider a continuous MAR with the
kernels $a_{ij}$ of {numref}`fig-ta-granger`. Here

$$
\operatorname{cov}(Y_1(t), Y_2(t-s)) = \int_0^\infty \big[a_{11}(u) a_{21}(u-s) + a_{12}(u) a_{22}(u-s)\big]\, du = 0
$$

for all integers $s \ge 1$, so past $Y_2$ does not help predict $Y_1$; yet because neither
$a_{11}$ nor $a_{12}$ is identically zero, $y_2$ *does* Granger-cause $y_1$ in continuous time. What
these constructions have in common is that most of the variance of $Y_1(t)$ originates in
innovations within the last sampling interval $[t-1, t)$.

```{figure} figures/fig-18-7.png
:name: fig-ta-granger
:width: 60%
:align: center

Continuous-time kernels for which $y_2$ Granger-causes $y_1$, but the sampled $Y_2$ does not
Granger-cause $Y_1$: the cross-covariances of $Y_1$ with past $Y_2$ vanish at all integer lags.
```

Unit-averaging can reverse the direction in yet another way. Take
$a_{11}(u) = e^{-\lambda u}$, $a_{21}(u) = K e^{-\lambda u}$, and $a_{12} \equiv 0$. In continuous
time $y_2$ does not Granger-cause $y_1$, and by Proposition 4 the same holds for the point-sampled
process. But if the first variable is unit-averaged while the second is sampled, the smoothing of
$a_{11}$ destroys the alignment that kept the cross-prediction zero, and $Y_2$ comes to
Granger-cause $Y_1$. Whether continuous-time non-causality survives aggregation thus depends not
only on the continuous dynamics but on exactly how the data are collected.

## 5. Unit-Averaged Data

Often the data are not point samples but *averages* over the sampling interval — a monthly figure
that is the average of the underlying weekly values, for example. Suppose the first variable is
observed as a unit average and the second as a point sample:

$$
Y_1(t) = \int_0^1 y_1(t-s)\, ds, \qquad Y_2(t) = y_2(t), \qquad t \in I.
$$

The averaged variable is itself a point sample of the continuous process
$\tilde{y}_1(t) = \int_0^1 y_1(t-s)\, ds = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \ell(u)\, y_1(t-u)\, du$, where
$\ell$ is the indicator of $[0,1)$. To apply the results of Section 3 we need the MAR of
$\tilde{y}_1$. Passing to the frequency domain, the spectral density of the filtered process is

$$
f_{\tilde{y}_1}(\omega) = \hat{\ell}(\omega)\, f_{y_1}(\omega)\, \hat{\ell}'(\omega)
= \big[\hat{\ell}(\omega)\, \hat{a}_1(\omega)\big]\big[\hat{\ell}(\omega)\, \hat{a}_1(\omega)\big]',
$$

where $\hat{\cdot}$ denotes the Fourier transform. Since $\ell$ has no zeros in the left half plane,
$\ell * a_1$ inherits the fundamental (no-left-half-plane-zeros) property and is therefore the MAR
kernel of $\tilde{y}_1$:

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-avg-kernel
\tilde{a}_1(u) = (\ell * a_1)(u) = \int_0^1 a_1(u-s)\, ds.
```

Averaging therefore *smooths* the kernel and shifts its mass one unit to the right.

```{admonition} Proposition 5
:class: note
If $a_{ij} \in L^1$ then $\tilde{a}_{ij}$ is continuous; if in addition $a_{ij} \in C^s$ then
$\tilde{a}_{ij} \in C^{s+1}$.
```

*Proof.* With $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x a_{ij}$, we have $\tilde{a}_{ij}(u) = F(u) - F(u-1)$, which
is continuous because $F$ is; differentiability is inherited the same way. $\blacksquare$

In particular $\tilde{a}(0) = 0$: an averaged process always has a continuous kernel that vanishes
at the origin. As Example 3.2 already warned, such kernels tend to produce distorted discrete MARs.
{numref}`fig-ta-avg` shows the typical configuration when $a$ is continuous, strictly positive for
$u > 0$, and $a(0) = 0$: the projection $h(u) = \mu\, a(u-1)$ leaves a large residual
$\int_1^2 c^2 = \int_1^2 (a-h)^2$ relative to $\int_0^1 c^2 = \int_0^1 a^2$, so that $A_k$ leans
more on values of $a$ in $[k+1, k+2)$ than in $[k, k+1)$.

**Mixing averaged and sampled data.** Suppose we estimate a two-variable discrete system in which
$Y_1$ is unit-averaged and $Y_2$ is point-sampled — a common situation when two series are recorded
at different frequencies. This practice systematically **overstates the importance of the sampled
variable** $Y_2$ in predicting the first.

The reason follows from the projection picture. Mixing the two kinds of data is equivalent to
sampling the continuous process $\hat{y}(t) = (\tilde{y}_1(t), y_2(t))'$, whose MAR kernel is
$\hat{a} = (\tilde{a}_1, a_2)'$ with $\tilde{a}_1$ the smoothed kernel {eq}`eq-ta-avg-kernel`. The
discrete forecast coefficients $\mu_k$ in $\eta(Y_1(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) = \sum_k \mu_k'\, Y(t-k)$ are
chosen to make $h_1(u) = \sum_k \mu_k'\, \hat{a}(u-k)$ as close as possible to the first kernel.

To isolate the role of the own-variable weights, suppose we set the $Y_2$-weights to zero and try
to approximate the first kernel using only its own integer shifts. For the *sampled* system the
target is $a_1$, and a combination $g(u) = \sum_k \lambda_k\, a_1(u-k)$ can match it fairly well,
because $a_1(0) \ne 0$ leaves the shifted copies room to reproduce the early part of $a_1$
({numref}`fig-ta-avg-sampled`). For the *averaged* system the target is instead $\tilde{a}_1$,
which vanishes at the origin and is smooth; a combination
$\hat{g}(u) = \sum_k \lambda_k\, \tilde{a}_1(u-k)$ approximates it much less well
({numref}`fig-ta-avg-averaged`), because the shifted copies $\tilde{a}_1(u-k)$ also start from zero
and cannot easily build up the mass that $\tilde{a}_1$ has just after each integer.

:name: fig-ta-avg-sampled

Since the $\mu_k$ are chosen to make $h_1$ as close to the first kernel as possible, the worse own
approximation in the averaged case means the first components of the $\mu_k$ — the own-variable
weights — shrink, and the burden of prediction shifts onto the sampled variable $Y_2$. The same
reasoning explains why, when only $Y_1$ is averaged, past $Y_1$ also becomes less effective at
predicting $Y_2$.

The simulations below support this. Several discrete-time bivariate AR(2) processes were aggregated
three ways: a **sampled** system (both variables sampled every three periods), an **averaged**
system (both averaged over three periods), and a **mixed** system (first averaged, second sampled).
A VAR was fit to each, and the variance decomposition computed for both Choleski orderings. The
prediction is that the share of variance explained by $Y_1$ should be smallest in the mixed system.

## 6. Sampling Ever More Finely

Finally, we ask whether the continuous model is recovered as the sampling interval shrinks. For
$\delta > 0$ define $Y^\delta(t) = y(t\delta)$, with discrete MAR
$Y^\delta(t) = \sum_k A_k^\delta\, \epsilon^\delta(t-k)$. A point of bookkeeping matters here: the
index $t$ in $Y^\delta(t)$ counts sampling dates, not real time — it corresponds to the real date
$t\delta$. To compare models with different $\delta$ we must therefore index information sets by
real time $q$, writing $H_{Y^\delta}(q)$ for the closed span of $\{Y^\delta(t) : t\delta \le q\}$
and $Y^\delta(t/\delta)$ for the observation at real date $t$ (assuming $t/\delta$ is an integer).
With this convention the natural question is whether continuous-time objects at a *fixed real
horizon* are recovered as $\delta \downarrow 0$.

The forecasts converge under very general conditions.

```{admonition} Proposition 6
:class: note
Under the maintained assumptions, for any $\alpha > 0$, $t$, and $i$,
$\eta(Y_i^\delta(t/\delta)\mid H_{Y^\delta}(t-\alpha)) \to \eta(y_i(t)\mid H_y(t-\alpha))$ as
$\delta \downarrow 0$.
```

*Proof.* By stationarity take $t = 0$. A continuity lemma underlies everything: using the MAR,
$E(y_i(t) - y_i(t+s))^2 = \sum_j \int_0^\infty |a_{ij}(u) - a_{ij}(u+s)|^2\, du \to 0$ as $s \to 0$,
because each square-integrable $a_{ij}$ satisfies $\int |a_{ij}(u) - a_{ij}(u+s)|^2 \to 0$; hence
$y(t+s) \to y(t)$ in mean square and the autocovariance function is continuous at the origin.

Now pick a finite combination $a_\nu = \sum_k (\lambda_k^\nu)'\, y(t_k^\nu)$, with all
$t_k^\nu < -\alpha$, that approximates $\eta(y_i(0)\mid H_y(-\alpha))$ within $\theta/2$. For each
$\delta$ replace each date $t_k^\nu$ by the nearest multiple of $\delta$, obtaining
$b_\delta \in H_{Y^\delta}(-\alpha)$. The continuity lemma gives $y(t_k^\delta) \to y(t_k^\nu)$, so
$b_\delta \to a_\nu$, and by the triangle inequality $d[b_\delta,\, \eta(y_i(0)\mid H_y(-\alpha))] < \theta$
for small $\delta$. Finally, the law of iterated projections,

$$
\eta(Y_i^\delta(0)\mid H_{Y^\delta}(-\alpha))
= \eta\big[\eta(y_i(0)\mid H_y(-\alpha)) \mid H_{Y^\delta}(-\alpha)\big],
$$

shows the discrete projection is at least as close to $\eta(y_i(0)\mid H_y(-\alpha))$ as $b_\delta$
is, giving the claim. $\blacksquare$

Convergence of the *coefficients* requires choosing the normalization carefully, and the choice is
genuinely delicate. If we insist that $\epsilon^\delta$ be the one-step innovation, then
$A_0^\delta = I$ for every $\delta$, while the continuous kernel has $a(0)$ that need not be $I$, so
the discrete MAR cannot converge to $a$. If instead we normalize the innovation to have variance
$I$, the MAR coefficients collapse to zero as $\delta \downarrow 0$ for *any* process, since the
innovation over a vanishing interval carries vanishing variance. The natural compromise mimics the
defining property of a continuous-time random measure — that it assigns covariance $\delta\, I$ to
an interval of length $\delta$. With $\xi^\delta$ the one-step innovation,

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-norm
\epsilon^\delta(t) = \delta^{1/2}\, W^{-1}\, \xi^\delta(t), \qquad W W' = E[\xi^\delta(t)\,\xi^\delta(t)']^{-1},
```

so that $E[\epsilon^\delta(k)\,\epsilon^\delta(k)'] = \delta\, I$, exactly as a random measure
assigns covariance $\delta\, I$ to an interval of length $\delta$. This is the normalization that
should be used when comparing the MARs of two series collected at different frequencies. With it,
the step-function $A^\delta$ built from the coefficients satisfies $\int_0^\infty \|A_i^\delta\|^2 = \int_0^\infty \|a_i\|^2$
and approximates $a$ in the following sense.

```{admonition} Proposition 7
:class: note
For any $\alpha > \beta \ge 0$ and $q \ge 0$,
$\int_\beta^\alpha A^\delta(u+q)\, A^\delta(u)'\, du \to \int_\beta^\alpha a(u+q)\, a(u)'\, du$ as
$\delta \downarrow 0$.
```

*Proof (for $\beta = 0$).* Fix $\alpha$ and $q$. By Proposition 6 and the Hilbert-space property
that convergent arguments pass through inner products,

$$
\rho_q^\delta \equiv E\big[y(q)\,(Y^\delta(0) - \eta(Y^\delta(0)\mid H_{Y^\delta}(-\alpha)))'\big]
\ \to\ E\big[y(q)\,(y(0) - \eta(y(0)\mid H_y(-\alpha)))'\big] \equiv \rho,
$$

and the right-hand side equals $\int_0^\alpha a(u+q)\, a(u)'\, du$ by the continuous MAR. On the
discrete side, expanding $Y^\delta$ in its $\delta$-MAR gives
$\rho_q^\delta = \int_0^\alpha A^\delta(u+q)\, A^\delta(u)'\, du$ (up to rounding $q$ to a multiple
of $\delta$, which is harmless as $\delta \downarrow 0$). Equating the two limits proves the case
$\beta = 0$; the general case follows from
$\int_\beta^\alpha = \int_0^\alpha - \int_0^\beta$. $\blacksquare$

That is, the impulse responses to innovations occurring over a fixed interval of *real* time are
approximated by the finely sampled model.

These results must be read with care, for two reasons. They fix an interval in real time *before*
letting $\delta \to 0$, and they depend on the normalization {eq}`eq-ta-norm`. If instead one keeps
$\epsilon^\delta$ equal to the one-step innovation — as is usual in applied work — and asks whether
that innovation, as $\delta \to 0$, becomes perfectly correlated with the continuous-time
innovation over the last interval of length $\delta$, the answer can be no.

```{admonition} Proposition 8
:class: note
Let $a$ be a one-dimensional continuous-time MAR with $a(0) = 0$, $a(u) > 0$ for small $u > 0$,
$a''(0+)$ existing, and $a'(\cdot-)$ bounded near zero. Then the correlation between the
one-step discrete innovation and the continuous-time innovation over the last interval does **not**
tend to one as $\delta \downarrow 0$.
```

This class is important: unit-averaging any process whose kernel has a right derivative at zero
produces a kernel satisfying the hypotheses. So the common practice of reading the one-step
innovation as the continuous-time innovation over a short interval can be misleading, however
finely the data are sampled.

## 7. Conclusion

The coefficients of the discrete, sampled MAR are

$$
A_k = \left[\int_0^\infty a(u+k)\, c(u)'\, du\right]\left[\int_0^\infty c(u)\, c(u)'\, du\right]^{-1},
$$

a weighted average of the continuous kernel $a$ with weighting kernel $c$. The single number that
governs the quality of the discrete representation is the size of $c$ on $[1,\infty)$, which equals
the gap between how well one can predict with discrete and with continuous data. When that gap is
small, $A_k$ is essentially an average of $a$ over $[k, k+1)$ and faithfully reflects the
continuous dynamics; when it is large, the discrete MAR can be badly distorted. A systematic
feature in all cases is that sampling inflates the first few MAR coefficients.

This gives the practitioner a way to reason about temporal-aggregation bias: knowing roughly how
much predictive power is lost by sampling, one can anticipate the magnitude of the distortion,
and ask whether a surprising estimated MAR could have been produced by the formula above from a
plausible continuous kernel. Unit-averaging compounds the problem — it smooths the kernel and
shifts its mass to the right — and mixing averaged with sampled data systematically overstates the
importance of the sampled variables. Finally, while finer sampling does recover the continuous-time
forecasts and, under the right normalization, the continuous-time impulse responses, it does not
rescue the interpretation of the one-step innovation as a short-horizon continuous-time innovation.
Convergence of the discrete Wold decomposition to the continuous one is studied further in Marcet
(1987).

Seen alongside Chapter 17, the message is a cautionary complement. There, working entirely in
continuous time, the projection onto the information set $H(t)$ had a transparent form on the
function side. Here the very same projection idea, applied to a process we can only sample, shows
how much of that transparency survives the passage to discrete data — and how the choice of
sampling scheme, far from being a mere matter of measurement, reshapes the dynamics, the causal
orderings, and the innovations that an econometrician will recover.

## Appendix: Autoregressive Representation of $Y$

Proposition 1 located $h_i$ as the projection of $a_i$ onto the closed span $A$, and hence as a
*limit* of finite combinations $\sum_k \mu_k'\, a(u-k)$. In fact the limit is attained: $h_i$ is
itself exactly such a combination.

```{admonition} Lemma 1
:class: note
For each $i$, there exist $\lambda_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $k = 1, 2, \dots$, with
$h_i(u) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda_k'\, a(u-k)$ for almost every $u$.
```

*Sketch.* Approximating functions $f_\nu(u) = \sum_k \mu_{k,\nu}'\, a(u-k)$ converge to $h_i$ in
$L_n^2$. On $[1,2)$ only the $k=1$ term survives (since $a$ vanishes on the negatives), so
$\int_0^1 \|(\mu_{1,\nu} - \mu_{1,m})\, a\|^2 \to 0$. Because $y$ is full rank, $\int_0^1 a a'$ is
positive definite, and this forces $\mu_{1,\nu}$ to converge to some $\lambda_1$. Iterating
interval by interval pins down $\lambda_2, \lambda_3, \dots$, and the limits agree with $h_i$
almost everywhere. $\blacksquare$

Applying the lemma to $\eta(Y(t)\mid H_Y(t-1)) = \int_1^\infty h(u)\,\zeta(t-du)$ and interchanging
the (now convergent) sum with the integral gives the **autoregressive representation**

```{math}
:label: eq-ta-arr
Y_i(t) = \varepsilon_i(t) + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda_k'\, Y(t-k),
```

whose coefficients are exactly the $\lambda_k$ of Lemma 1. The interchange of sum and integral is
legitimate whenever the coefficient sequence is well behaved.

```{admonition} Lemma 2
:class: note
If $\{\lambda_k\}$ is absolutely summable, then {eq}`eq-ta-arr` holds.
```

*Sketch.* By the triangle inequality in $L_n^2$,
$\big[\int_1^\infty \|\sum_{k \ge m} \lambda_k'\, a(u-k)\|^2\big]^{1/2} \le K \sum_{k \ge m} |\lambda_k|$,
which vanishes as $m \to \infty$ when $\{\lambda_k\}$ is absolutely summable. $\blacksquare$

Thus, under a mild summability condition, the sampled process has the autoregressive representation
{eq}`eq-ta-arr`, with autoregressive coefficients determined — through the projection of $a$ onto
its own integer shifts — by the continuous-time kernel.
